\section*{Introduction}
Category theory is used here as a language for organizing constructions that
are already familiar, not as a separate prerequisite subject. The central
questions are elementary: What kinds of objects are being studied? Which maps
are allowed between them? Does a construction respect those maps? This
appendix develops only the vocabulary needed elsewhere in these notes:
categories, isomorphisms, opposite categories, functors, commutative diagrams,
and natural transformations.

The pacing is intentionally concrete. Each abstract definition separates its
typed data from its axioms, and examples explain what the symbols record.
Yoneda's lemma, adjunctions, representable-functor theory, limits and colimits,
abelian categories, exact or derived functors, enriched categories, monads,
and Kan extensions lie outside the present scope. The forms examples at the
end may be read after Chapter~13; they organize results proved there and do
not supply prerequisites for that chapter.

\section{Objects, Morphisms, and Composition}
A category records objects, morphisms between them, and how those morphisms
compose. Sets and functions give the first model, but the language is useful
because the permitted morphisms can encode continuity, linearity, smoothness,
an order relation, or some other structure.

\begin{definition}[Category]\label{def:category}
A \textbf{category} $\mathcal C$ consists of the following data.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*]
\item A class of objects, denoted
\[
\operatorname{Obj}(\mathcal C).
\]
We say ``class'' because the objects of an important category need not form a
set.

\item For each ordered pair
\[
A,B\in\operatorname{Obj}(\mathcal C),
\]
a set of morphisms
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C}(A,B).
\]
Writing $f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C}(A,B)$ is abbreviated to
\[
f:A\longrightarrow B.
\]
Here $A$ is the source or domain of $f$, while $B$ is its target or codomain.
The actual range is a separate notion when morphisms happen to be functions.

\item For every triple of objects $A,B,C$, a composition map
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C}(B,C)
\times\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C}(A,B)
\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C}(A,C),
\]
\[
(g,f)\longmapsto g\circ f.
\]
Thus
\[
A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C
\]
produces $g\circ f:A\to C$. The notation means first $f$, then $g$.

\item For every object $A$, an identity morphism
\[
1_A:A\longrightarrow A.
\]
\end{enumerate}

These data satisfy two axioms.

\emph{Associativity.} For composable arrows
\[
A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C\xrightarrow{h}D,
\]
one has
\[
h\circ(g\circ f)=(h\circ g)\circ f.
\]

\emph{Identity laws.} For every $f:A\to B$,
\[
1_B\circ f=f,
\qquad
f\circ1_A=f.
\]
\end{definition}

For ordinary functions, associativity says that both parenthesizations
perform the same three evaluations in the same order. Identity functions do
nothing. A category keeps precisely these compositional features even when
its arrows are not presented as functions between displayed underlying sets.

\subsection{Four familiar categories}
The following table gives the first examples to keep in mind.
\[
\begin{array}{c|l|l}
\text{category}&\text{objects}&\text{morphisms}\\ \hline
\mathbf{Set}&\text{sets}&\text{functions}\\
\mathbf{Vect}_{\R}&\text{real vector spaces}&\text{linear maps}\\
\mathbf{Top}&\text{topological spaces}&\text{continuous maps}\\
\mathbf{Man}&\text{smooth manifolds without boundary}&\text{smooth maps}
\end{array}
\]
In each row, composition and identities are the ordinary ones. The defining
property of the morphisms is preserved by composition: composites of linear,
continuous, or smooth maps remain linear, continuous, or smooth.

For this appendix, $\mathbf{Man}$ uses smooth Hausdorff second-countable
manifolds \emph{without boundary}. Chapter~13 develops the broader
boundary-capable setup. That broader class is important for Stokes' theorem,
but it is not needed for the categorical examples here.

\begin{example}[Divisibility as a category]
Let the objects be the positive integers. Declare that there is one arrow
\[
a\longrightarrow b
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
a\mid b,
\]
and no arrow otherwise. Reflexivity $a\mid a$ gives the identity arrow. If
$a\mid b$ and $b\mid c$, transitivity gives $a\mid c$, hence composition.
For example,
\[
2\longrightarrow6\longrightarrow30
\]
composes to the arrow $2\to30$.

There is at most one arrow between any two objects. Therefore two composites
with the same endpoints must agree, proving associativity and the identity
laws. The arrow $2\to6$ is not being treated as a function from one displayed
integer to another; it records the divisibility relation.
\end{example}

More generally, every poset $(P,\leq)$ gives a category with one arrow
$x\to y$ exactly when $x\leq y$. Reflexivity and transitivity become the
identity and composition laws. Antisymmetry says that arrows in both
directions force equality of objects. In the poset of subsets of a fixed set,
the arrows are inclusions: $A\subseteq B\subseteq C$ gives the composite
inclusion $A\to C$.

\subsection{Isomorphism is not equality}
\begin{definition}[Isomorphism]\label{def:categorical-isomorphism}
A morphism
\[
f:A\longrightarrow B
\]
is an \textbf{isomorphism} if there exists a morphism
\[
g:B\longrightarrow A
\]
such that
\[
g\circ f=1_A,
\qquad
f\circ g=1_B.
\]
The morphism $g$ is called an inverse of $f$.
\end{definition}

The inverse is unique. Indeed, if $g$ and $h$ are both inverses, then
\[
g=g\circ1_B=g\circ(f\circ h)
=(g\circ f)\circ h=1_A\circ h=h.
\]
We may therefore write $f^{-1}$ and write $A\cong B$ when an isomorphism
exists.

In the four familiar categories, the isomorphisms are respectively
bijections, linear isomorphisms, homeomorphisms
(\cref{def:homeomorphism}), and diffeomorphisms. A continuous bijection need
not be an isomorphism in $\mathbf{Top}$: the identity map from $\R$ with the
discrete topology to $\R$ with its usual topology is continuous and
bijective, but the inverse is not continuous. The category determines which
structure the inverse must preserve.

Literal equality $A=B$ and isomorphism $A\cong B$ are different assertions.
For example,
\[
T:\R^2\longrightarrow\{p\in\R[t]:\deg p\leq1\},
\qquad
T(a,b)=a+bt,
\]
is a linear isomorphism. The two vector spaces are nevertheless not literally
the same set with the same presentation. Moreover, this particular
isomorphism uses the displayed coordinates and polynomial basis.

\begin{remark}[Canonical, natural, and noncanonical]
The following working distinctions will be enough here:
\[
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\text{canonical}
&:\text{ determined by the stated data without arbitrary auxiliary choices},\\
\text{natural}
&:\text{ compatible with morphisms in the relevant variables},\\
\text{noncanonical}
&:\text{ requires additional choices}.
\end{aligned}}
\]
The words are related but are not synonyms. Naturality is the formal
compatibility condition defined later. Canonical is a working description of
how an object or map is determined. For finite-dimensional $V$, the
evaluation map $V\to V^{**}$ is determined without choosing a basis and is
natural; an isomorphism $V\cong\R^n$ requires a basis and is noncanonical.
Canonical does not mean ``the only isomorphism,'' and the bare existence of
some isomorphism gives no preferred choice.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The opposite category}
Contravariant constructions reverse arrows. The opposite category turns that
reversal into ordinary covariant bookkeeping.

\begin{definition}[Opposite category]\label{def:opposite-category}
The \textbf{opposite category} $\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}$ has the same
objects as $\mathcal C$:
\[
\operatorname{Obj}(\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}})
=\operatorname{Obj}(\mathcal C).
\]
For objects $A,B$, define
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}}(A,B)
=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C}(B,A).
\]
Thus an arrow $f:A\to B$ in $\mathcal C$ gives a formally reversed arrow
\[
f^{\mathrm{op}}:B\longrightarrow A
\]
in $\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}$.

Suppose
\[
A\xrightarrow{f^{\mathrm{op}}}B
\xrightarrow{g^{\mathrm{op}}}C
\]
in $\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}$. The underlying arrows in $\mathcal C$ are
$C\xrightarrow{g}B\xrightarrow{f}A$, so composition is defined by
\[
\boxed{
g^{\mathrm{op}}\circ_{\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}}f^{\mathrm{op}}
=(f\circ_{\mathcal C}g)^{\mathrm{op}}.}
\]
The identity of $A$ is the same underlying $1_A$.
\end{definition}

The order reverses because the underlying arrows point the other way. The
identity laws in $\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}$ are the identity laws in
$\mathcal C$ read backward. Likewise, for three composable opposite arrows,
the two parenthesizations reduce to the two equal parenthesizations in
$\mathcal C$; hence associativity holds. A contravariant construction on
$\mathcal C$ is therefore an ordinary covariant functor from
$\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}$.

\subsection{A brief size convention}
A category is \textbf{small} if its objects and all its morphisms form sets.
It is \textbf{locally small} if
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C}(A,B)
\]
is a set for every fixed pair of objects $A,B$. Familiar categories such as
$\mathbf{Set}$, $\mathbf{Top}$, and $\mathbf{Vect}_{\R}$ are usually locally
small rather than small: their collections of all objects are too large to be
sets, but the maps between two fixed objects form a set. No further
foundational size theory is needed in these notes.

\section{Functors and Variance}
A construction becomes substantially more useful when it tells us not only
what happens to objects, but also what happens to maps between those objects.
For example, forgetting topology sends a topological space to its underlying
set and sends a continuous map to the same underlying function. The action on
maps is not extra decoration: it is what makes the construction compatible
with changing the input.

Another elementary example assigns
\[
P(X)=X\times X
\]
to a set $X$. A function $f:X\to Y$ determines
\[
P(f):X\times X\longrightarrow Y\times Y,
\qquad
P(f)(x_1,x_2)=(f(x_1),f(x_2)).
\]
Identity functions leave both coordinates unchanged. If
$X\xrightarrow{f}Y\xrightarrow{g}Z$, then both $P(g\circ f)$ and
$P(g)\circ P(f)$ send $(x_1,x_2)$ to
$(g(f(x_1)),g(f(x_2)))$. This is the pattern abstracted next.

\begin{definition}[Covariant functor]\label{def:functor}
A \textbf{covariant functor}
\[
F:\mathcal C\longrightarrow\mathcal D
\]
consists of two assignments.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*]
\item To every object $A$ of $\mathcal C$, it assigns an object $F(A)$ of
$\mathcal D$:
\[
A\longmapsto F(A).
\]
\item To every morphism $f:A\to B$ in $\mathcal C$, it assigns a morphism
in $\mathcal D$ with the displayed domain and codomain:
\[
f:A\to B
\quad\longmapsto\quad
F(f):F(A)\to F(B).
\]
\end{enumerate}
These assignments satisfy
\[
F(1_A)=1_{F(A)}
\]
for every object $A$, and
\[
F(g\circ f)=F(g)\circ F(f)
\]
for every composable pair $A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C$.
\end{definition}

The forgetful construction $U:\mathbf{Top}\to\mathbf{Set}$ satisfies the
axioms because the underlying function of an identity is an identity and the
underlying function of a composite is the composite of the underlying
functions. The product construction $P$ above satisfies them by the explicit
coordinate calculation. Functors also preserve isomorphisms: applying $F$ to
the two inverse equations shows that $F(f^{-1})$ is inverse to $F(f)$.

The direct-image construction gives another example. It sends a set $X$ to
its power set $\mathcal P(X)$ and a function $f:X\to Y$ to
\[
f_*:\mathcal P(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal P(Y),
\qquad
A\longmapsto f(A).
\]
The identities
\[
(1_X)_*(A)=A,
\qquad
(g\circ f)_*(A)=g_*(f_*(A))
\]
verify the functor laws elementwise.

\subsection{Contravariant functors}
Variance records arrow direction. The two patterns are
\[
\begin{array}{c@{\qquad}c}
\text{covariant}&\text{contravariant}\\[1mm]
f:A\to B&f:A\to B\\
\Downarrow&\Downarrow\\
F(f):F(A)\to F(B)&F(f):F(B)\to F(A).
\end{array}
\]

\begin{definition}[Contravariant functor]
A \textbf{contravariant functor} $F$ from $\mathcal C$ to $\mathcal D$
assigns an object $F(A)$ to each object $A$ and, to each arrow $f:A\to B$,
an arrow
\[
F(f):F(B)\longrightarrow F(A).
\]
It satisfies
\[
F(1_A)=1_{F(A)}
\]
and, for $A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C$,
\[
\boxed{F(g\circ f)=F(f)\circ F(g).}
\]
Equivalently, it is an ordinary covariant functor
$\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathcal D$.
\end{definition}

The algebraic dual is the basic example. A linear map $T:V\to W$ induces
\[
T^*:W^*\longrightarrow V^*,
\qquad
T^*(\lambda)=\lambda\circ T.
\]
The identity law follows from $\lambda\circ1_V=\lambda$. If
$V\xrightarrow{T}W\xrightarrow{S}U$, then for $\mu\in U^*$,
\[
(S\circ T)^*(\mu)=\mu\circ S\circ T
=T^*(S^*(\mu)),
\]
so
\[
(S\circ T)^*=T^*\circ S^*.
\]
The reversal is forced by precomposition. The pullback of differential forms
will follow the same pattern.

\section{Commutative Diagrams and Natural Transformations}
A diagram is \textbf{commutative} when composites along directed paths with
the same starting and ending objects are equal. The diagram is a compact
record of a typed equation.

For the triangle
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
A \arrow[rr,"f"] \arrow[dr,"h"'] && B \arrow[dl,"g"]\\
& C &
\end{tikzcd}
\]
the two paths from $A$ to $C$ are the direct arrow $h$ and the composite
$g\circ f$. Commutativity means
\[
g\circ f=h.
\]

For the square
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
A \arrow[r,"f"] \arrow[d,"u"'] & B \arrow[d,"v"]\\
C \arrow[r,"g"'] & D,
\end{tikzcd}
\]
the top-then-right path gives $v\circ f:A\to D$, while the left-then-bottom
path gives $g\circ u:A\to D$. The square commutes exactly when
\[
v\circ f=g\circ u.
\]
A commutative diagram summarizes an equality of composites; it does not
replace the proof of that equality.

For a concrete square, let $P(X)=X\times X$ and define the diagonal
\[
\Delta_X:X\longrightarrow X\times X,
\qquad
\Delta_X(x)=(x,x).
\]
Given $f:X\to Y$, both routes around
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
X \arrow[r,"\Delta_X"] \arrow[d,"f"'] & X\times X \arrow[d,"P(f)"]\\
Y \arrow[r,"\Delta_Y"'] & Y\times Y
\end{tikzcd}
\]
send $x$ to $(f(x),f(x))$. This is not one accidental square: it is a
compatible comparison for every set and every function.

\subsection{Naturality}
If $F$ and $G$ are two constructions on the same category, what does it mean
to compare them compatibly with every morphism? A natural transformation
answers this question.

\begin{definition}[Natural transformation]\label{def:natural-transformation}
Let
\[
F,G:\mathcal C\longrightarrow\mathcal D
\]
be covariant functors. A \textbf{natural transformation}
\[
\eta:F\Rightarrow G
\]
assigns to each object $A$ a component morphism
\[
\eta_A:F(A)\longrightarrow G(A).
\]
For every morphism $f:A\to B$, these components satisfy the naturality
equation
\[
\boxed{G(f)\circ\eta_A=\eta_B\circ F(f).}
\]
Equivalently, the square
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
F(A) \arrow[r,"\eta_A"] \arrow[d,"F(f)"'] &
G(A) \arrow[d,"G(f)"]\\
F(B) \arrow[r,"\eta_B"'] & G(B)
\end{tikzcd}
\]
commutes.
\end{definition}

In words: change the input first and then compare, or compare first and then
change the input; the result is the same. The diagonal maps above give a
natural transformation from the identity functor on $\mathbf{Set}$ to the
product functor $P$. The elementwise calculation proves the required square
for an arbitrary function $f$.

Naturality is stronger than choosing an unrelated morphism
$F(A)\to G(A)$ for each object. The components must cooperate with every
allowed change of input. This is the precise sense in which ``natural'' is a
compatibility property rather than merely a synonym for ``familiar'' or
``canonical.''

\section{Differential Forms as the Analytic Payoff}
The abstract language now packages constructions already proved in
Chapter~13. For a smooth map
\[
f:M\longrightarrow N,
\]
pullback gives a linear map
\[
f^*:\Omega^k(N)\longrightarrow\Omega^k(M).
\]
The direction reverses. Identity and composition satisfy
\[
(1_M)^*=1_{\Omega^k(M)},
\qquad
(g\circ f)^*=f^*\circ g^*.
\]
Consequently
\[
\boxed{\Omega^k:\mathbf{Man}^{\mathrm{op}}
\longrightarrow\mathbf{Vect}_{\R}}
\]
is a contravariant functor. In degree zero, $f^*u=u\circ f$ is ordinary
precomposition; the reversal is already visible there.

Exterior differentiation supplies a comparison between two such functors:
\[
d:\Omega^k\Rightarrow\Omega^{k+1}.
\]
Its component at a manifold $M$ is
$d_M:\Omega^k(M)\to\Omega^{k+1}(M)$. For $f:M\to N$, the relevant
contravariant naturality square is
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
\Omega^k(N) \arrow[r,"d_N"] \arrow[d,"f^*"'] &
\Omega^{k+1}(N) \arrow[d,"f^*"]\\
\Omega^k(M) \arrow[r,"d_M"'] & \Omega^{k+1}(M).
\end{tikzcd}
\]
Starting with $\omega\in\Omega^k(N)$, the top-then-right route gives
$f^*(d\omega)$, while the left-then-bottom route gives $d(f^*\omega)$.
Thus commutativity is exactly the identity
\[
\boxed{f^*(d\omega)=d(f^*\omega),}
\]
proved in \cref{prop:manifold-exterior-derivative}.

Appendix~\ref{app:cohomology} constructs de Rham cohomology and the maps
induced by pullback. Those results give the contravariant functor
\[
H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}:
\mathbf{Man}^{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Vect}_{\R}.
\]
Appendix~\ref{app:cohomology}, not the present appendix, supplies the
cohomology construction and verifies that pullback descends to it. The role
of the present language is organizational: one formula records the objects,
the reversed arrows, identities, and composition laws together.

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}
Let $P$ be a poset. Verify separately the object data, morphism sets,
composition, identities, associativity, and identity laws for its category.
Show that an arrow $x\to y$ is an isomorphism exactly when $x=y$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{hexercise}{app-b-2}
In the divisibility category, determine every isomorphism. Then explain why
the arrows $2\to6$ and $6\to30$ compose although they are not functions
between the displayed integers.
\end{hexercise}

\begin{hexercise}{app-b-3}
Suppose $A\xrightarrow{f^{\mathrm{op}}}B
\xrightarrow{g^{\mathrm{op}}}C$ in $\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}$. Write the
corresponding arrows in $\mathcal C$ and compute their composite in
$\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}$, including its source and target.
\end{hexercise}

\begin{exercise}
For the functor $P(X)=X\times X$, verify the identity and composition laws
directly on an arbitrary ordered pair. Then prove that both coordinate
projections $P\Rightarrow\operatorname{Id}_{\mathbf{Set}}$ are natural.
\end{exercise}

\begin{hexercise}{app-b-5}
Prove the two contravariant functor identities for the algebraic dual
$V\mapsto V^*$. Track every domain and codomain before comparing the
composites.
\end{hexercise}

\begin{hexercise}{app-b-6}
Check the naturality square for the diagonal maps
$\Delta_X:X\to X\times X$ elementwise. State in words what the two routes do
to an arbitrary $x\in X$.
\end{hexercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional real vector space. Explain why choosing a basis
gives an isomorphism $V\cong\R^n$ but does not give a canonical one. Contrast
this with the evaluation map $V\to V^{**}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{hexercise}{app-b-8}
For a smooth map $f:M\to N$, write the directions of the four arrows in the
naturality square for exterior differentiation. Explain why a form on $N$
pulls back to $M$, rather than the other way around.
\end{hexercise}
